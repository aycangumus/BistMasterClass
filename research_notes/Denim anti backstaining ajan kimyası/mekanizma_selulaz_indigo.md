## 1. What is the physical/chemical state of indigo in the wash bath, and how does it differ from leuco-indigo?

### Takeaway
In an enzyme/stone wash bath indigo is present as the oxidised, water-insoluble, non-ionic pigment (C16H10N2O2, CAS 482-89-3), not as a dissolved dye: it is a particulate/colloidal dispersion whose agglomerate size is not fixed but is shifted by the proteins in the bath (acid *Trichoderma* cellulases keep it finely dispersed, neutral *Humicola* cellulases agglomerate it). Direct measurements of the particle-size distribution and zeta potential of abraded indigo in a real denim wash liquor were **not** found; the only numeric colloid data located are for surfactant-stabilised indigo pigment dispersions (150–200 nm, −24 to −32 mV).

### Cited Findings
- **Insoluble pigment form.** Patent and review literature on denim washing treat backstaining explicitly as "redeposition of insoluble dye, such as insoluble indigo dye" [P/S] — [US 6,146,428 / related enzyme-treatment patents, USPTO](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6146428); the academic adsorption work is likewise framed as affinity of cellulases "for insoluble indigo dye" (Campos et al. 2000) [A] — [Campos, Cavaco-Paulo, Andreaus, Gübitz, Textile Research Journal 70(6):532–536, 2000, DOI 10.1177/004051750007000610](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000610).
- **Why it is insoluble.** Indigo's insolubility in water, dilute alkali and dilute acid is attributed to intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonding (N–H···O=C); the molecule is planar, "insoluble in water and has no affinity for the fiber" in its oxidised form [S, low-tier trade/educational sources] — [Fibre2Fashion, "Indigo dyeing: an overview"](https://www.fibre2fashion.com/industry-article/7331/indigo-dyeing-an-overview); [Asian Textile Studies, "Indigo"](http://www.asiantextilestudies.com/indigo.html).
- **Water-solubility figures conflict.** ChemicalBook's property table lists water solubility "<0.1 g/100 mL", but the same page's text gives "1.6 % (water) at 25 °C"; the latter contradicts the rest of the literature and should not be used. The predicted pKa on that page (−3.83) refers to neutral indigo, not leuco-indigo [S, low-tier] — [ChemicalBook, Indigo](https://www.chemicalbook.com/ChemicalProductProperty_EN_CB7459700.htm).
- **Leuco-indigo contrast.** Leuco-indigo is the reduced, water-soluble anionic form obtained only under strongly reducing conditions at pH ≈ 11 or above; its ionisation increases with pH; at dyeing pH both leuco-indigo and cotton carry negative charge, which lowers affinity; higher temperature reduces leuco-indigo affinity for cotton [S, low-tier] — [Fibre2Fashion](https://www.fibre2fashion.com/industry-article/7331/indigo-dyeing-an-overview); [TextileTuts, "Denim dyeing with indigo dyes"](https://textiletuts.com/denim-dyeing-with-indigo-dyes/). None of the wash-bath conditions (pH 4.5–7, 40–60 °C, air-saturated, no reducing agent) allow the leuco form to exist, so wash-bath indigo is the neutral pigment.
- **Agglomerate size depends on the enzyme.** "The particle size of indigo dye agglomerates is influenced by cellulase origin and concentration" (2000) [A] — [Campos et al., TRJ 70(6):532–536](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000610). Crude neutral cellulases from *Humicola insolens* appear to agglomerate the dye particles (and reduce backstaining, with a visible increase of blue/white contrast), whereas acid cellulases from *Trichoderma reesei* disperse the dye particles [S/U — reported in search snippets tied to the same paper's listing] — [ResearchGate record of Campos et al. 2000](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220030789_Indigo-Cellulase_Interactions). A 2003 review by the same group restates that cellulase proteins "interact with indigo, reduce indigo particle size and act as carriers of fine indigo particles to the fabric", and that *H. insolens* neutral cellulases at low concentration seem to promote agglomeration [S] — [Tzanov, Andreaus, Guebitz, Cavaco-Paulo, "Protein interactions in enzymatic processes in textiles", Electronic Journal of Biotechnology 6(3), December 2003](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0717-34582003000300013).
- **Laccase also shifts the size distribution.** After laccase treatment the indigo agglomerates "seemed to have shifted to smaller diameters for all laccases", the effect depending on laccase origin and incubation time (2001) [A] — [Campos, Kandelbauer, Robra, Cavaco-Paulo, Gübitz, Journal of Biotechnology 89(2–3):131–139, 2001, DOI 10.1016/S0168-1656(01)00303-0, PMID 11500206](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11500206/).
- **Only numeric colloid data located (proxy, not wash liquor).** Indigo pigment dispersions stabilised with a polyoxyethylene lauryl ether dispersant showed particle sizes of 150–200 nm and zeta potentials of about −24 to −32 mV (study aimed at inks/printing, not denim washing) [U — value appeared in the search summary; the exact source document among the results could not be pinned down; candidates:] — [RSC Green Chemistry author manuscript, "Environment-friendly nanocellulose-indigo dyeing of textiles"](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/getauthorversionpdf/d1gc02043a); [US 9,803,315](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/9803315).
- **Surface charge argument (qualitative).** One source explains redeposition by "the less charged surface of cotton that enhanced the affinity of the negatively charged indigo to cotton" (i.e. indigo particles are treated as weakly negative, and acid pH lowers the cotton surface charge) [U — snippet-level; originating paper not identified, candidates:] — [Maryan & Montazer, "Nanoclay as new tool for discoloration of dyed denim garment with indigo"](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257815304_Nanoclay_as_New_Tool_for_Discoloration_of_Dyed_Denim_Garment_with_Indigo); [Patra, Madhu, Bala 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-017-0126-9).
- **Hydrophobicity is the property the patent literature leans on.** Conventional dye-transfer-inhibiting polymers "are not effective in preventing the backstaining of indigo dyes due to the extreme hydrophobicity of indigo dyes" [P] — [US 6,337,313, "Textile manufacturing and treating processes comprising a hydrophobically modified polymer"](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6337313); repeated in [US 8,618,039 B2, "Granular materials for finishing denim"](https://patents.google.com/patent/US8618039B2/en). A later application words it as a supposition: PVP and poly(vinylpyridine-N-oxide) "cannot prevent backstaining of indigo in particular, which may be due to the extreme hydrophobicity of this dye" [P] — [US 2008/0028539 A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20080028539A1/en).
- **Binding chemistry with proteins.** Indigo binds strongest to immobilised tyrosine and phenylalanine among amino acids immobilised on agarose (2000) [A] — Gusakov, Sinitsyn, Berlin, Markov, Ankudimova, Enzyme and Microbial Technology 27(9):664–671, 2000 — [journal index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/76854755); the companion conference paper states that indigo's aromatic rings can interact with Tyr, Trp and Phe through hydrophobic contacts [A/S] — [Gusakov et al., Biocatalysis-2000 proceedings, Vestnik Moskovskogo Universiteta, Khimiya, 2000 supplement](https://org321-5.chem.msu.ru/eng/journals/vmgu/00add/77.pdf). Indigo–cellulase binding is described as weak and driven by hydrophobic interactions and hydrogen bonds [S] — [Tzanov et al. 2003](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0717-34582003000300013).

### Inferences
- Because the bath contains pigment particles rather than dissolved dye, backstaining is a **particle-deposition / hetero-coagulation and adsorption problem**, not a dye-exhaustion equilibrium; classical dye-transfer inhibitors that work by 1:1 complexation of dissolved dye molecules (PVP, PVP-NO) have nothing to complex. This is consistent with the patent statements above, though the patents themselves offer hydrophobicity only as the probable reason.
- The fact that bath proteins move the agglomerate size in both directions (dispersion by *T. reesei* enzymes, agglomeration by *H. insolens* enzymes; smaller agglomerates after laccase) implies that the "native" particle size of abraded indigo is not a constant of the system — any measurement or agent design must specify the protein/surfactant environment.
- Indigo offers both H-bond donors/acceptors (N–H, C=O) and a planar aromatic surface; an agent that is to hold it in the bath needs a hydrophobic/aromatic anchoring segment plus a strongly hydrated (or charged) stabilising segment. The affinity for Tyr/Phe suggests aromatic or π-rich anchors are particularly suitable (inference from Gusakov 2000, not tested there for polymers).

### Gaps
- **No primary measurement** of particle-size distribution, zeta potential vs pH, isoelectric point, contact angle or Hansen solubility parameters of indigo abraded from denim in a wash liquor was found. The −24/−32 mV and 150–200 nm values are for dispersant-stabilised pigment and their source needs re-identification.
- Measured log P/log Kow and a reliable aqueous solubility for indigo were not retrieved (ChemicalBook is internally inconsistent).
- Leuco-indigo ionisation constants were not retrieved. *Unverified lead from memory:* the Etters-group literature on indigo dyeing (e.g. J. N. Etters, Textile Chemist & Colorist/American Dyestuff Reporter, 1990s) gives two pKa values for leuco-indigo (commonly quoted ≈ 8.0 and ≈ 12.7) and the mono-phenolate as the high-substantivity species — verify before use.

## 2. What are the proposed backstaining mechanisms and what evidence supports each?

### Takeaway
The peer-reviewed literature (1998–2003, Minho/Graz and Moscow groups) converges on a **protein-mediated** picture: cellulase protein adsorbed on cotton (via its cellulose-binding domain) and indigo's affinity for that protein are the main drivers, with indigo–protein binding being weak, non-specific, hydrophobic/aromatic and H-bond based. The groups disagree on the direction of the dose effect (Moscow: more adsorbed protein → more backstaining; Minho: more fungal cellulase on the fabric → less staining, and indigo sitting on adsorbed cellulase is *easier* to wash off than indigo bound directly to cotton), which shows that **direct indigo–cotton deposition also exists and is the more tenacious form**. Mechanical entrapment is not addressed as a separate mechanism in any source retrieved.

### Cited Findings
**(b) Cellulase-mediated ("bridge/carrier") mechanism — evidence**
- 1998, Minho/UBC: analysing staining on different cotton surfaces during enzyme stone-washing, the authors concluded that "the high ability of the cellulase enzyme protein to bind to cotton cellulose is the major cause of backstaining"; the pH of maximum cellulase activity is not necessarily the pH of maximum binding to the substrate; indigo has "completely different affinities for cellulase proteins from different fungal origins" [A] — [Cavaco-Paulo, Morgado, Almeida, Kilburn, "Indigo backstaining during cellulase washing", Textile Research Journal 68(6):398–401, 1998, DOI 10.1177/004051759806800602](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/004051759806800602).
- 1998, Moscow (Gusakov/Sinitsyn): a small-scale model microassay for backstaining was developed and 11 cellulase samples were ranked; the paper's thesis is in its title — interaction between indigo and *adsorbed protein* is a major factor causing backstaining; protein adsorption on the white yarn is described as the crucial factor [A/S] — [Gusakov, Sinitsyn, Berlin et al. (eight authors, incl. Emalfarb), Applied Biochemistry and Biotechnology 75:279–293, 1998, DOI 10.1007/BF02787781](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02787781).
- 2000, Minho/Graz: adsorption studies showed that acid cellulases from *T. reesei* have a higher affinity for insoluble indigo than neutral cellulases from *H. insolens* [A] — [Campos et al., TRJ 70(6):532–536](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000610).
- 2000, Minho/Graz: deletion of the cellulose-binding domain (CBD) from either bacterial or fungal cellulases decreases indigo staining and "generally causes less backstaining than the entire enzyme" [A] — [Andreaus, Campos, Gübitz, Cavaco-Paulo, "Influence of cellulases on indigo backstaining", Textile Research Journal 70(7):628–632, 2000, DOI 10.1177/004051750007000711](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000711). The 2003 review adds that whole enzymes with a binding domain show more affinity to indigo and cause more backstaining than the core enzyme without CBD [S] — [Tzanov et al. 2003](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0717-34582003000300013).
- 2001, Moscow: binding of several cellulases from *T. reesei*, *Penicillium verruculosum* and *Chrysosporium lucknowense* to indigo particles and to Avicel was compared; much higher amounts of protein bound to indigo than to cellulose under similar conditions; adsorption on indigo was less specific than on cellulose; the three endoglucanases with the highest indigo-binding ability gave the best denim-washing performance; protein adsorption on cotton is a crucial parameter for backstaining for both crude preparations and purified components [A] — [Gusakov, Sinitsyn, Markov, Sinitsyna, Ankudimova, Berlin, "Study of protein adsorption on indigo particles confirms the existence of enzyme–indigo interaction sites in cellulase molecules", Journal of Biotechnology 87(1):83–90, 2001 (PII S0168-1656(01)00234-6)](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168165601002346).
- 2000, Moscow: for six purified fungal cellulases (four endoglucanases, two cellobiohydrolases) "a certain correlation" exists between denim-washing performance and the share of aromatic residues exposed on the protein surface / the overall percentage of surface hydrophobic residues; Tyr and Phe immobilised on agarose bound indigo most strongly; hypothesis: cellulases with surface clusters of non-polar residues bind indigo and act as emulsifiers that help the dye float out of the fibre [A; the authors' wording is qualified — correlation, not proof] — Gusakov, Sinitsyn, Berlin, Markov, Ankudimova, "Surface hydrophobic amino acid residues in cellulase molecules as a structural factor responsible for their high denim-washing performance", Enzyme and Microbial Technology 27(9):664–671, 2000 — [journal index record](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/76854755); conference version arguing that only cellulases with such "indigo-binding domains" remove indigo efficiently — [Vestnik MGU Khimiya 2000 suppl.](https://org321-5.chem.msu.ru/eng/journals/vmgu/00add/77.pdf).
- 2013 (Chinese group): more cellulase protein bound to cotton gave higher backstaining, read by the authors as direct adsorption between cellulase and indigo [S/U — snippet-level] — ["Influences of Acid Cellulase on Indigo Backstaining on Cotton", Advanced Materials Research 798–799:35, 2013](https://www.scientific.net/AMR.798-799.35).
- Indirect industrial evidence: adding a protease during treatment with a "redepositing cellulase" gave reflectance readings significantly different from the redepositing-cellulase control and similar to a non-redepositing cellulase (comparator: Denimax L, a *Humicola* endoglucanase, at 2.5 mL product/L = 100 ppm total protein) [P] — [WO 1994/029426 A1 / EP 0702713 B1, "Enzymatic compositions and methods for producing stonewashed look on indigo-dyed denim fabric"](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO1994029426A1/en). A *Trichoderma* cellulase given a limited protease treatment and then purified gave "a dramatic reduction in the level of backstaining and a visible increase in the contrast between white and blue threads" [P] — [US 5,700,686](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5700686A/en). Both results are what the protein-bridge model predicts (destroying or clipping the protein removes the stain carrier).

**(a) Direct deposition of indigo on the fibre — evidence**
- After-washing experiments on indigo-stained cotton showed "it is easier to remove indigo adsorbed on cellulase adsorbed onto cotton than indigo directly adsorbed onto cotton"; and increasing concentrations of fungal cellulase on the fabric *decrease* staining (2000) [A] — [Andreaus et al., TRJ 70(7):628–632](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000711). This demonstrates a direct indigo–cotton deposition pathway that does not need the enzyme and is more wash-resistant.
- In the Novozymes cutinase patent, polyester and polyester/cotton swatches (10 cm × 10 cm) washed in an indigo bath without any protective enzyme gave L* values of only about 64–69 (heavy staining), and the cellulase itself showed no anti-backstaining effect on the swatches [P] — [US 6,797,010, Novozymes A/S, issued 28 Sep 2004](https://patents.justia.com/patent/6797010). Polyester has no cellulose for a CBD to bind, so this is direct deposition on a hydrophobic fibre.
- The non-ionic form of indigo behaves like a disperse dye with substantivity for polyester (2004) [A/S] — ["An approach to the dyeing of polyester fiber using indigo and its extended wash fastness properties", Dyes and Pigments, 2004](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0143720803002237).

- No retrieved source isolates mechanical entrapment of particles in the yarn/fabric structure as a mechanism. The nearest experimental handle is that Campos et al. (2000) examined how different levels of mechanical action affect cellulase adsorption on undyed versus indigo-dyed fabric [A] — [TRJ 70(6):532–536](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000610) — but the direction/magnitude was not available at abstract level.

**Where the sources disagree**
- *Dose direction:* Gusakov et al. (1998, 2001) and the 2013 AMR paper tie more adsorbed protein to more backstaining — [ABAB 75:279–293](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/BF02787781); Andreaus et al. - *Indigo binding is double-edged:* in the Moscow work high indigo-binding ability correlates with the *best abrasion/washing performance* (enzyme as emulsifier lifting dye) — [J. ### Inferences
- A consistent synthesis (my inference, not stated as such by any single source): three pools of indigo exist on the white yarn — (i) indigo bound to cotton-adsorbed cellulase (loosely held, removable by post-wash, protease, surfactant), (ii) indigo deposited directly on cellulose (tenacious), (iii) indigo deposited on hydrophobic fibres/finishes (polyester pocketing, labels, sewing thread, elastane). Free (non-adsorbed) protein in the bath acts as a dispersant/carrier; protein adsorbed on the fibre acts as an acceptor layer. Whether more enzyme helps or hurts then depends on the bound/free ratio and on whether protein-covered cotton is a better or worse acceptor than bare cotton — which reconciles Andreaus (2000) with Gusakov (1998).
- The CBD result means an anti-backstaining agent can act on two interfaces: (1) keep indigo away from the protein/fibre (dispersant action on the particle) and (2) reduce or loosen cellulase adsorption on the white yarn (competitive adsorption on the fibre), without killing catalytic activity.
- "Carrier of fine particles" + "agglomeration reduces backstaining" imply that very small protein-coated indigo particles are the most depositable species; an agent need not solubilise indigo — changing the particle surface (steric layer) or its size class may suffice.

## 3. Why do acid cellulases backstain more than neutral and EG-enriched cellulases? Role of pH, temperature, time, liquor ratio, mechanical action, dose and CBD

### Takeaway
Three experimentally supported reasons: (1) acid *T. reesei* cellulase proteins have a higher affinity for indigo than neutral *H. insolens* proteins; (2) *T. reesei* enzymes keep indigo finely dispersed whereas *H. insolens* enzymes agglomerate it; (3) whole *T. reesei* preparations are rich in CBD-carrying proteins that adsorb strongly on cotton, while low-backstaining enzymes are typically CBD-free small endoglucanases (e.g. *Melanocarpus albomyces* Cel45A) or core/truncated enzymes. Whether pH acts directly (fibre/particle charge) or only indirectly (through enzyme activity and binding) is disputed. Quantitative data on temperature, time, liquor ratio and mechanical action were not retrieved.

- The pH of maximum activity may differ from the pH of maximum binding to cotton (1998) [A] — [Cavaco-Paulo et al., TRJ 68(6):398–401](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/004051759806800602).

**Endoglucanase-enriched and mono-component enzymes**
- Among purified *T. reesei* EG I, EG II and CBH I and two mixtures from engineered strains, EG II was the most effective at removing colour from denim (2000) [A] — [Heikinheimo, L., Buchert, J., Miettinen-Oinonen, A., Suominen, P., "Treating denim fabrics with *Trichoderma reesei* cellulases", Textile Research Journal 70(11):969–973, 2000, DOI 10.1177/004051750007001106](https://cris.vtt.fi/en/publications/treating-denim-fabrics-with-itrichoderma-reeseii-cellulases/).
- *T. reesei* strains over-producing EG II (one with the *cbh2* locus replaced by *egl2*; endoglucanase activity up to four-fold above host) gave preparations more efficient in stonewashing than the parental strain; purified EG II gave a good stonewashing effect at the lowest hydrolysis level (2002) [A] — [Miettinen-Oinonen, A., Suominen, P., Applied and Environmental Microbiology 68(8):3956–3964, 2002, DOI 10.1128/AEM.68.8.3956-3964.2002, PMID 12147496](https://journals.asm.org/doi/10.1128/aem.68.8.3956-3964.2002). (Backstaining values from this paper were not retrievable.)
- *Melanocarpus albomyces*: the 20 kDa endoglucanase (Cel45A, GH45) is relatively heat-stable with a high pH optimum and performs well in neutral biostoning; adding the purified 50 kDa endoglucanase (Cel7A) or the 50 kDa cellobiohydrolase to the 20 kDa enzyme *decreased* backstaining (2004) [A] — ["Three cellulases from *Melanocarpus albomyces* for textile treatment at neutral pH", Enzyme and Microbial Technology, 2004](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S014102290300365X). None of these three cellulases carries a cellulose-binding domain, and *T. reesei*-produced Cel45A preparations "caused considerably less backstaining as compared to the acid cellulase product of *T. reesei*" at neutral pH (2004) [A] — ["Cloning of cellulase genes from *Melanocarpus albomyces* and their efficient expression in *Trichoderma reesei*", Enzyme and Microbial Technology, 2004](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0141022903003326). Structure: GH45 six-stranded β-barrel like *H. insolens* EG V, 2.0 Å — [PDB 1OA9](https://www.rcsb.org/structure/1OA9).
- *Chrysosporium lucknowense*: the commercial preparation had greater biostoning potential than *T. reesei* in the microassays, and a ≈25 kDa endoglucanase was identified as the key enzyme for strong abrasion (2001) [A] — [Sinitsyn, Gusakov, Grishutin, Sinitsyna, Ankudimova, "Application of microassays for investigation of cellulase abrasive activity and backstaining", Journal of Biotechnology 89(2–3):233–238, 2001, DOI 10.1016/S0168-1656(01)00306-6, PMID 11500217](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11500217/).
- Across 29 cellulase preparations there was no direct relationship between abrasive (denim-washing) activity and saccharification ability (2000) [A] — [Gusakov et al., Applied Biochemistry and Biotechnology 88:119–126, 2000](https://rd.springer.com/article/10.1385/ABAB%3A88%3A1-3%3A119).

**CBD**
- CBD deletion lowers staining for both bacterial and fungal cellulases; family I (fungal) CBD enzymes: more enzyme → less staining; family II (bacterial) CBD enzyme: concentration has no effect (2000) [A] — [Andreaus et al., TRJ 70(7):628–632](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000711). Core enzymes without a cellulose-binding module decrease indigo backstaining [S] — [Springer chapter 2017](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-10-2474-0_3).

**Dose / time / load**
- Enzyme amount, treatment time and load size are named as practical backstaining levers but without numbers [S] — [Cotton Incorporated ISP-1003](https://www.cottoninc.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/ISP-1003-Enzyme-Technology-for-Cotton-Products.pdf).
- Mechanical action changes cellulase adsorption on undyed vs indigo-dyed fabric (studied, direction not retrieved) [A] — [Campos et al. 2000](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000610).

### Inferences
- The low-backstaining enzyme archetype emerging from the data is: **small, single-domain (no CBD) endoglucanase of GH45 (and, by industrial analogy, GH12), operating at pH 6–8**. It combines weak, reversible adsorption on cotton with a protein surface that agglomerates rather than disperses indigo.
- Two results caution against an oversimplified "indigo-binding = bad" rule: (i) EG types that bind indigo best abrade best (Gusakov 2001), and (ii) Cel45A alone backstains more than Cel45A + Cel7A/CBH (Miettinen-Oinonen 2004) — additional non-adsorbing protein in the bath seems to act as a sacrificial indigo acceptor/dispersant. An anti-backstaining auxiliary can be thought of as a synthetic replacement for that "free protein" function.
- If pH acted only through enzyme identity, an acid enzyme used with an effective dispersant should approach neutral-enzyme backstaining; the protease/"non-redepositing" comparison in WO 94/29426 supports that this is achievable in principle.

- No retrieved quantitative data (K/S, reflectance, ΔE vs. variable) on the effects of **temperature, time, liquor ratio and mechanical action** on backstaining; the planned search could not be run. The full texts of Klahorst et al. 1994, Kochavi et al. 1990 and Sarıışık 2004 are the likely sources.

## 4. Indigo affinity to different fibres (cotton, polyester, polyamide, elastane) and to proteins; why is polyester pocketing/label staining severe?

### Takeaway
Oxidised indigo is a non-ionic, hydrophobic, disperse-dye-like species: it has documented substantivity for polyester (it can even be dyed into PET at 90–130 °C) and binds non-specifically and abundantly to proteins via aromatic/hydrophobic residues. Patent data show polyester and polyester/cotton swatches stain heavily in an indigo bath (L* ≈ 64–69) and that making the polyester surface less hydrophobic with a cutinase raises L* to ≈ 81–90, which points to hydrophobic particle–fibre interaction as the cause. No comparative study covering polyamide and elastane was found.

### Cited Findings
- **Polyester as a substrate for indigo.** Both conventional and microfibre polyester were dyed with indigo at 90–130 °C; colour strength increased with temperature; full penetration was confirmed on fibre cross-sections (2005) [A] — ["Indigo adsorption properties to polyester fibers of different levels of fineness", Dyes and Pigments, 2005](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0143720804001639). The non-ionic form of indigo, like a disperse dye, has substantivity for polyester, whereas the non-ionic and leuco-acid forms have substantivity for cellulose (2004) [A/S] — ["An approach to the dyeing of polyester fiber using indigo and its extended wash fastness properties", Dyes and Pigments, 2004](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0143720803002237).
- **Polyester swatch staining and its reversal by surface hydrolysis (patent data, pH and doses as given in snippets).** Polyester washed in an indigo bath: L* 65.4 without enzyme → 84.6 with 10 mg/L "Cutinase A" → ≈ 89.7 at 50 mg/L; low-dose series 63.7 → 81.2 at 5 mg/L; at pH 5, 68.5 → 81.9 at 30 mg/L. Cutinases and lipases showed an anti-backstaining effect on polyester and polyester/cotton, including at acid pH; cellulase alone showed none on these swatches [P] — [US 6,797,010, "Redeposition or backstain inhibition during stonewashing process", Novozymes A/S, 2004](https://patents.justia.com/patent/6797010); family members [EP 1290129 B1](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP1290129B1/en), [US 2005/0055780 A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20050055780A1/en).
- **Polyester-specific anti-redeposition chemistry exists.** A separate application claims preventing or minimising dye redeposition "by use of polyesters" (polyester-type polymers), and states PVP/PVNO do not stop indigo backstaining [P] — [US 2008/0028539 A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20080028539A1/en); [EP 2009087 A2](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2009087A2/en).
- **Where the stain is seen.** Backstaining is described as soiling of the weft thread and the pocket lining by detached indigo; combined laccase–cellulase processes are evaluated on "the back of the garment and the white pocket" [A/S] — [Patra et al. 2018](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1186/s40691-017-0126-9); [Montazer & Sadeghian Maryan, "Influences of different enzymatic treatment on denim garment", Applied Biochemistry and Biotechnology, 2010, DOI 10.1007/s12010-009-8727-4](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12010-009-8727-4).
- **Proteins.** Much more cellulase protein binds to indigo particles than to Avicel cellulose, and less specifically (2001) [A] — [Gusakov et al., J. Biotechnol. 87:83–90](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168165601002346). Strongest binding to immobilised Tyr and Phe (2000) [A] — [Gusakov et al., Enzyme Microb. Technol. 27:664–671 (index record)](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/76854755). *T. reesei* proteins > *H. insolens* proteins in indigo affinity (2000) [A] — [Campos et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000610).
- **Cotton.** Indigo deposited directly on cotton is harder to remove than indigo sitting on cotton-adsorbed cellulase (2000) [A] — [Andreaus et al.](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000711).
- **Other synthetics (titles only, content not retrieved).** "Dyeing nylon with indigo in various pH regions" — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236232448_Dyeing_Nylon_with_Indigo_in_Various_pH_regions); "A study on the exhaust dyeing of various synthetic fibers with indigo" — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/236268487_A_Study_on_the_Exhaust_Dyeing_of_Various_Synthetic_Fibers_with_Indigo); "Dyeing of polyester fabrics with indigo", Research Journal of Textile and Apparel 15(4), 2011 — [Emerald](https://www.emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/RJTA-15-04-2011-B003/full/html).

### Inferences
- On polyester the cellulase-CBD bridge cannot operate (no cellulose), so pocketing/label staining is evidence for mechanism (a): hydrophobic attraction between a non-ionic pigment particle (or its hydrophobic protein/surfactant coat) and a low-energy, weakly charged fibre surface. At wash temperatures of 40–60 °C (below the glass-transition range of PET) this should be surface deposition rather than the in-fibre diffusion seen at 90–130 °C — which is why it is partly removable but also why any heat applied later (drying, curing) risks fixing it. (Inference; not measured in any retrieved source.)
- The cutinase result implies the design lever for polyester is the **fibre surface**, not only the particle: an agent that adsorbs on polyester and presents a hydrated layer (soil-release-polymer logic: PET-anchor + PEG/POE blocks) should reduce staining; this is consistent with the existence of the "use of polyesters" patent, whose mechanism statement was not retrieved.
- For a universal agent: cotton white-yarn protection calls for control of protein/particle adsorption on cellulose; pocketing protection calls for hydrophilisation of polyester — two different interfaces, plausibly two different active components.

### Gaps
- No comparative, quantitative study of indigo redeposition on **cotton vs polyester vs polyamide vs elastane** in a denim wash was found; nothing at all on elastane; nylon only as a dyeing-study title.
- The statement that polyester stains *more* than cotton in the wash bath is supported only by the patent swatch data and trade experience, not by a peer-reviewed side-by-side measurement retrieved here.
- Numerical values in US 6,797,010 were read from search snippets (enzyme doses, pH, exact table structure) and need checking against the patent.
- Author/volume/page details of the two Dyes and Pigments papers were not displayed (believed: Son, Hong, Kim, Dyes Pigm. 61, 2004; Son, Lim, Hong, Kim, Dyes Pigm. 65, 2005) — verify.

## 5. Which classes of agents reduce backstaining, by what claimed mechanism, and how effective are they?

### Takeaway
The best-documented academic result is for ethoxylated nonionic surfactants: > 75 % reduction of indigo adsorption at < 0.2 g/L, together with reduced cellulase adsorption on cotton and no loss (actually a gain) in cellulase weight-loss activity; PVP works in the same direction but is less efficient, and the patent literature states that classical dye-transfer polymers (PVP, PVNO) do not stop indigo backstaining, motivating hydrophobically modified polymers. Enzymatic routes — protease (destroys the protein carrier), cutinase/lipase (hydrophilises polyester; L* +17 to +24 units in patent tests), laccase (destroys bath indigo and bleaches redeposited indigo) — and clay sorbents (montmorillonite scavenges indigo) are each supported by at least one experimental source. Data on EO degree/HLB optimisation, anionic dispersants, polyacrylates and sequestrants were not found.

### Cited Findings
**Nonionic surfactants (ethoxylates)**
- With *Hypocrea jecorina* (= *T. reesei*) cellulases: indigo adsorption "was successfully reduced by more than 75 % with ethoxylated nonionic surfactants at concentrations below 0.2 g/L"; cotton weight loss after 120 min was significantly *increased* by nonionic surfactants and by polyvinylpyrrolidone; protein adsorption on cotton decreased with increasing additive concentration, the nonionic surfactants being more efficient than PVP (published online 14 Dec 2012; issue 2013) [A] — [Zilz, L., Rau, M., Budag, N., Scharf, M., Cavaco-Paulo, A., Andreaus, J., "Nonionic surfactants and dispersants for biopolishing and stonewashing with *Hypocrea jecorina* cellulases", Coloration Technology 129(1):49–54, 2013, DOI 10.1111/cote.12003](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cote.12003/pdf).
- In after-washing of stained fabric, nonionic surfactants and dispersing agents were the most efficient agents, and severe post-washing helps [S/U — snippet-level, attributed to the Minho group's work] — [repository record of the Minho backstaining papers](https://www.rcaap.pt/vufind/PublicationRecord/rum_93caa2a5debe8a5a0300e7cd0786c54b).

### Inferences
- Mechanistic reading of Zilz et al. (2013): the nonionic ethoxylate works on **both** interfaces — it lowers (non-productive) cellulase adsorption on cotton, removing the protein acceptor layer, and it lowers indigo adsorption; because cellulase weight loss rises at the same time, an anti-backstaining ethoxylate does not have to trade off against abrasion. This is the single strongest first-principles justification for an ethoxylate-based (or POE-bearing polymer) agent.
- The patent line "PVP-type DTI polymers fail → hydrophobically modified hydrophilic polymers work" maps onto the standard anchor–buoy dispersant architecture: hydrophobe anchors on the indigo particle (or on polyester), hydrophilic backbone provides steric/electrosteric repulsion. Requirements that follow: anchor affinity for a planar aromatic H-bonding solid; stabilising chains soluble at pH 4.5–7 and 40–60 °C (below cloud point for ethoxylates); no inhibition of cellulase.
- Agent classes sort by target: (i) particle — nonionic surfactants, hydrophobically modified polymers, clays (sorbent sink); (ii) protein bridge — protease, CBD-free enzymes, surfactant competition; (iii) fibre surface — cutinase/lipase and polyester-type polymers for PET; (iv) chromophore destruction — laccase/mediator, peroxide, hypochlorite (also bleach the face, so they change the shade and are not pure anti-redeposition agents).
- Protease during the wash competes with abrasion (it digests the cellulase), which is why the literature places it at the end of the cellulase step or in the rinse.

## 6. Which colloid-science principles govern anti-redeposition of indigo?

### Takeaway
No retrieved source analyses indigo backstaining explicitly in DLVO/steric-stabilisation terms or reports the measurements (zeta potential of indigo and fibres vs pH, interaction energies, adsorption isotherms of agents on indigo) that such an analysis needs. What the experimental literature does establish maps onto four principles: competitive adsorption at the fibre (surfactant displaces protein/particles), modification of the particle surface and size class (protein or polymer coat; agglomeration vs fine dispersion), failure of molecular dye complexation for a pigment-like hydrophobic dye, and removal of the particle from the system by a sorbent sink or by chemical destruction.

### Cited Findings
- **Competitive adsorption on the fibre.** Ethoxylated nonionic surfactants and PVP reduce cellulase-protein adsorption on cotton in a concentration-dependent way, and the nonionics cut indigo adsorption by > 75 % at < 0.2 g/L (2013) [A] — [Zilz et al., Coloration Technology 129(1):49–54](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cote.12003/pdf).
- **Particle size class matters; proteins set it.** Enzymes that agglomerate indigo (*H. insolens*) backstain less than enzymes that keep it finely dispersed (*T. reesei*); cellulases are described as carriers of fine indigo particles to the fabric (2000, 2003) [A/S] — [Campos et al. 2000](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000610); [Tzanov et al. 2003](https://www.scielo.cl/scielo.php?script=sci_arttext&pid=S0717-34582003000300013).
- **Adsorbed layers on the particle.** Proteins adsorb on indigo in large amounts and non-specifically, through hydrophobic/aromatic contacts and H-bonds; cellulases with non-polar surface clusters are proposed to act as emulsifiers for indigo (2000, 2001) [A] — [Gusakov et al. 2001](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0168165601002346); [Gusakov et al. 2000 (index record)](https://acnpsearch.tweb-dev.unibo.it/singlejournalindex/76854755).
- **Bound layer vs direct contact.** Indigo held on a cellulase layer on cotton is easier to remove than indigo in direct contact with cotton (2000) [A] — [Andreaus et al. 2000](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/004051750007000711).
- **Electrostatics (qualitative only).** Redeposition is said to be stronger in acid than in neutral medium [S] — [Springer chapter 2017](https://link.springer.com/chapter/10.1007/978-981-10-2474-0_3); one source attributes this to the lower surface charge of cotton enhancing the affinity of negatively charged indigo [U] — [candidate source](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/257815304_Nanoclay_as_New_Tool_for_Discoloration_of_Dyed_Denim_Garment_with_Indigo); dispersant-stabilised indigo pigment showed zeta potentials of −24 to −32 mV [U] — [candidate source](https://pubs.rsc.org/en/content/getauthorversionpdf/d1gc02043a).
- **Molecular complexation is the wrong tool.** Dye-transfer-inhibiting polymers that complex dissolved direct dyes (PVP, PVNO) do not prevent indigo backstaining; hydrophobically modified hydrophilic polymers are claimed instead [P] — [US 2008/0028539 A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20080028539A1/en); [US 6,337,313](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6337313).
- **Fibre-surface energy.** Enzymatic hydrolysis of the polyester surface (cutinase/lipase) strongly reduces indigo deposition on polyester (L* 65 → 85–90) [P] — [US 6,797,010](https://patents.justia.com/patent/6797010).
- **Sorbent sink / chemical destruction.** Montmorillonite adsorbs bath indigo and lowers backstaining (2015) [A] — [Maryan et al., J. Clean. Prod. 91:208–215](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S0959652614013031?via=ihub); laccase degrades bath indigo and reduces staining on back and pocket (2001–2010) [A/S] — [Campos et al. 2001](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11500206/); [Montazer & Sadeghian Maryan 2010](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s12010-009-8727-4).

### Inferences
*(All of the following are derivations from general colloid science applied to the cited observations; none is demonstrated for indigo in the retrieved literature.)*
- **Electrostatic (DLVO) stabilisation is intrinsically weak in this system.** Indigo is non-ionic; any charge is acquired (adsorbed ions, protein, anionic dispersant). At pH 4.5–5.5 cotton's carboxyl-derived negative charge is partly suppressed and adsorbed cellulase (if near its isoelectric point) carries little net charge, so the electrostatic barrier to particle–fibre contact is low; the bath also contains buffer salts that compress the double layer. This is a plausible physical basis for "acid medium → more redeposition", in parallel with the enzyme-identity explanation; the two cannot be separated with the data found.
- **Steric / electrosteric stabilisation is the robust route.** A dispersant with an anchor for the indigo surface (aromatic/alkyl hydrophobe, H-bond acceptor groups for N–H) and well-hydrated chains (POE, polycarboxylate at pH ≥ 5.5) gives a repulsive barrier that is insensitive to pH and salt — matching the performance of ethoxylates and the patent move to hydrophobically modified hydrophilic polymers.
- **Competitive adsorption at the acceptor surface** is as important as stabilising the particle: Zilz et al. show the agent lowers protein adsorption on cotton, and the cutinase patent shows altering the polyester surface alone gives a large effect. A complete agent therefore needs a fibre-side function (surfactant/polymer that adsorbs on cotton and on polyester and hydrates the surface).
- **Micellar solubilisation** of indigo is unlikely to be significant at < 0.2 g/L surfactant given indigo's strong crystal lattice (H-bonded, insoluble even in most organic solvents); the > 75 % effect at such low concentration is more consistent with adsorption-layer effects than with solubilisation. (Inference; no solubilisation data found.)
- **Complexation by polymer** (PVP-type) fails because the target is a particle, not a molecule; however PVP still showed some benefit in Zilz et al. via reduced protein adsorption on cotton — so "PVP is ineffective" (patents) and "PVP helps somewhat" (Zilz) are not strictly contradictory: they address different steps.
- **Size-class engineering**: since agglomerated indigo backstains less, controlled flocculation onto a carrier (clay, large polymer flocs) that is too large to enter the yarn and rinses out is an alternative to fine dispersion; montmorillonite works this way as a sorbent sink.
- **Design requirements derivable from the evidence**: (1) active at pH 4.5–7 and 40–60 °C, in the presence of buffer salts and pumice fines; (2) does not inhibit, and preferably enhances, cellulase (nonionics did); (3) anchors on a hydrophobic, aromatic, H-bonding particle; (4) provides hydration/steric repulsion; (5) reduces cellulase–cotton non-productive adsorption or masks protein-coated cotton; (6) hydrophilises polyester pocketing/labels (soil-release function); (7) low foam in drum washers; (8) rinses out without fixing indigo.
