## 1. PVP, PVP/VI, PVNO and poly(vinylpyridine betaine) — the classical dye-transfer-inhibitor (DTI) polymers

### Takeaway
These polymers are built to complex dissolved, mostly anionic (direct/reactive) dyes at ppm level through H-bond-acceptor, dipolar and hydrophobic interactions, with an optimum molecular weight of roughly 10,000–70,000. For particulate, non-ionic, hydrophobic indigo the denim-specific evidence is mixed: one denim patent calls PVP and PVNO ineffective, while PVP still appears as a co-component in Chinese anti-backstaining formulations and in a Coloration Technology study.

### Cited Findings
**Molecular weight and grades**
- Ashland lists PVP K-15 with K-value 13–19 and MW 9,700; K-30 with K-value 26–35 and MW 66,800; K-90 with K-value 88–100 and MW 1,570,000 (GPC/MALLS; the average type was not stated in the snippet) — [Ashland PVP K-series](https://ashland.com/industries/personal-and-home-care/home-i-and-i/pvp-k-series)
- A third-party listing gives much lower "average" values of 10,000 / 40,000 / 160,000 / 360,000 for K-15 / K-30 / K-60 / K-90 (K = Fikentscher value). This conflicts with the Ashland figures because the measurement basis differs **(attribution not pinned)** — [Ataman PVP K-90 page](https://www.atamanchemicals.com/povidone-pvp-k-90_u32427/?lang=EN)
- Ashland's Agrimer brochure explains that K-values come from kinematic viscosity and that one polymer has different Mn, Mv and Mw. Its K-30 example is Mn 10,000, Mv 40,000, Mw 55,000 — [Ashland Agrimer PVP brochure](https://www.ashland.com/file_source/Ashland/Documents/PHA19-020%20Agrimer%20PVP%20Brochure.pdf)
- Detergent patents give preferred PVP DTI molecular weights of 5,000–400,000, preferably 5,000–200,000, more preferably 5,000–50,000, and in one patent most preferably 5,000–15,000 **(attribution not pinned within this patent set)** — [US 5,849,684](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5849684A/en); [WO 2003/020862](https://patents.google.com/patent/WO2003020862A1/en); [US 6,521,582](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6521582)

**Binding mechanism**
- PVP binding of the anionic dyes rose bengal and tiron (1979): comparison of binding constants pointed to hydrophobic interactions as important — [Maruthamuthu, J. Polym. Sci. Polym. Chem. Ed. 1979](https://doi.org/10.1002/pol.1979.170171008)
- A 2012 Korean study of ionic dyes with PVP reported binding that is mainly enthalpy-driven, with hydrophobic and hydrogen-bond contributions — [KSDF 2012](https://journal.kci.go.kr/ksdf/archive/articlePdf?artiId=ART001725001)
- For cross-linked PVP with anionic azo dyes, the listed binding modes are hydrophobic effects, dipole–induced-dipole and dipole–dipole forces, hydrogen bonds and ion–ion interactions — [Adsorption of textile dyes onto crosslinked PVP](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/244738712_Adsorption_of_Some_Textile_Dyes_onto_Crosslinked_Poly_N_-Vinylpyrrolidone)
- A review states that polymers complexing ionic dyes hold them in solution and so prevent redeposition **(attribution not pinned between two reviews)** — [Boardman, Rev. Prog. Color. 2000](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1478-4408.2000.tb03782.x); [Formulation of colour-care and heavy-duty detergents: a review](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233573140_Formulation_of_colour-care_and_heavy-duty_detergents_A_review)

**Dye selectivity and surfactant interference**
- Oakes, Gratton & Dixon (Coloration Technology 2003): the polymers were "extremely effective at inhibiting adsorption of most of the direct dyes to cotton but are less effective at inhibiting adsorption of small, model azo dyes" — [Oakes, Gratton & Dixon](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230138419_Adsorption_of_dyes_to_cotton_and_inhibition_by_surfactants_polymers_and_surfactant-polymer_mixtures)
- Same paper: "anionic surfactants at sub-micellar concentrations can inhibit polymer-dye interactions due to displacement of dye and/or relocation into micelle-like polymer–surfactant complexes". All surfactants inhibit dye adsorption above their CMC by taking the dye into micelles — [Oakes, Gratton & Dixon](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/230138419_Adsorption_of_dyes_to_cotton_and_inhibition_by_surfactants_polymers_and_surfactant-polymer_mixtures)
- Patent literature states that detergents with PVP plus anionic surfactant usually show weaker dye-transfer inhibition than PVP plus nonionic surfactant **(attribution not pinned)** — [EP 0753566](https://data.epo.org/publication-server/rest/v1.2/patents/EP0753566NWB1/document.pdf)
- Amin & Blackburn (ACS Sustainable Chem. Eng. 2015): for hydrolysed reactive dye removal, poly(vinylpyridine betaine) polymers were highly efficient and PVP polymers the least effective — [White Rose eprint](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/84731/3/Nabeel%20-%20SCE%20Paper%202015%20-%20revised2.pdf)

**Indigo-specific evidence**
- US 6,537,327 (denim stonewashing): "Conventional anti-dye transfer polymers such as polyvinylpyrrolidone and polyvinylpyrridine-N-oxide are effective for preventing the redeposition of direct dyes that are typically used on cotton. However, such conventional anti-dye transfer polymers are not effective in preventing the backstaining of indigo dyes due to the extreme hydrophobicity of indigo dyes." — [US 6,537,327](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6537327)
- Zilz et al. (Coloration Technology 2013): in cellulase treatment, protein adsorption on cotton fell as additive concentration rose, with nonionic surfactants more efficient than PVP. Cotton weight loss after 120 min rose significantly with both nonionic surfactants and PVP — [Zilz et al. 2013](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cote.12003/pdf)
- Chinese patent CN102392374B claims an enzyme-wash anti-backstaining agent containing alkali-hydrolysed styrene/maleic anhydride copolymer, 2–35 % PVP, 2–10 % fatty amine polyoxyethylene ether and 5–25 % fatty alcohol polyoxyethylene ether, balance water — [CN102392374B](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN102392374B/en)
- A denim bleaching patent describes PVP as a dispersing agent that reduces redeposition of stripped dye **(attribution not pinned; the only bleaching patent in the result set)** — [US 5,749,923](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5749923)
- US 5,730,760 (Kirk et al.) claims dye-deposition-inhibiting polymers for prewashing/stonewashing made of 5–100 wt % vinyl amide monomer and 0–95 wt % vinyl ester monomer — [US 5,730,760](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5730760)
- A later Ciba patent uses Sokalan HP 56 and PVP K-30 as the "standard commercial" anti-deposition benchmarks — [US 7,659,354](https://patents.justia.com/patent/7659354)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE]** Repeat-unit arithmetic: the PVP unit C6H9NO is 111.14 g/mol, so K-15 (9,700) is about 87 units, K-30 (Mw 55,000–66,800) about 500–600 units and K-90 (1.57 × 10^6) about 14,000 units. The PVNO unit C7H7NO is 121.14 g/mol, so 50,000 is about 410 units.
- **[INFERENCE]** These polymers work in laundry because the dye is molecularly dissolved, anionic and present at ppm level, so a 1:n polymer–dye complex competes with the fibre. In a denim bath indigo is a non-ionic crystalline particle at roughly g/L level (see section 9), and its N–H donors are already used in intra- and intermolecular H-bonds. The lactam C=O or N-oxide cannot "dissolve" indigo; the polymer can only act as a protective colloid adsorbed on the particle surface. Fully hydrophilic PVP and PVNO have weak hydrophobic anchoring, which fits the US 6,537,327 statement.
- **[INFERENCE]** The conflict is real and should be reported. A patent with a commercial interest in alternative polymers says PVP/PVNO do not work. A peer-reviewed study (Zilz) finds PVP reduces cellulase-protein adsorption on cotton, though less than ethoxylates. Chinese formulations use PVP only together with a hydrophobe-bearing polymer and ethoxylates. The Kirk patent's vinyl amide/vinyl ester copolymer is in effect a hydrophobically modified PVP (PVP/VA type). Taken together, the lactam group is a useful secondary binding group but needs a hydrophobic co-anchor to be effective on indigo.
- **[INFERENCE]** Molecular weight for denim use: the "no further gain above ~20,000" result and the patent optimum of 10,000–50,000 suggest K-15 to K-30. K-90 adds viscosity and bridging-flocculation risk without a binding gain.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** The pyridine-N-oxide group stays unprotonated and fully active across pH 4.5–7 (conjugate-acid pKa about 0.8) and its aromatic ring allows π-stacking with planar dyes. PVNO is generally regarded as less sensitive to anionic surfactant than PVP.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Poly(N-vinylimidazole) units have a pKa of roughly 5–6, so PVP/VI is partly cationic at acid-cellulase pH 4.5–5.5. That would raise its substantivity to negatively charged cotton and polyester and to anionic co-dispersants. **[INFERENCE]** PVP/VI is therefore better suited to the neutral-cellulase window (pH 6–7) than to the acid one.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** The pyridinium carboxymethyl betaine is zwitterionic above about pH 3, so it carries no net charge at pH 4.5–7.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** PVP-type polymers are used as levelling/stripping agents for vat and direct dyes in textile dyeing, which implies genuine affinity for (leuco) vat dyes. This lead is worth verifying.

### Gaps
- No quantitative PVP–dye, PVNO–dye or PVP/VI–dye binding constants were retrieved. The Oakes papers and the Jäger & Denzinger Tenside papers could not be opened.
- No binding or adsorption data for PVP, PVNO or PVP/VI with indigo, indigo carmine, vat or disperse dyes were found.
- Molecular weight of Sokalan HP 56 / HP 66 is not confirmed. The technical specification gave only viscosity and solids.
- Chromabond S-403E (PVNO) data sheet not found; molecular weight and solids of S-100 and S-403E are unknown. The PVNO CAS number was not verified.
- The examples and backstaining data of US 6,537,327 and US 5,730,760 were not retrieved, so the "PVP ineffective" claim rests on a patent background statement only.

## 2. Polycarboxylates (sodium polyacrylate; acrylic/maleic copolymers)

### Takeaway
Polycarboxylates are well documented as dispersants for inorganic particulates and as Ca/Mg sequestrants, but no source found here shows that an unmodified polycarboxylate disperses indigo or prevents backstaining. Their value in a denim bath is as a secondary component (pumice fines, hardness) and as the hydrophilic backbone of hydrophobically modified polymers, which do carry denim claims.

### Cited Findings
- Sokalan CP 5 is a maleic acid–acrylic acid copolymer sodium salt with molecular weight 70,000, K value 60 and viscosity 2,800 cP (liquid grade) — [ChemPoint, Sokalan CP 5](https://www.chempoint.com/products/basf/sokalan-acrylic-polymers/sokalan-acrylic-polymers/sokalan-cp-5)
- The BASF granule grade lists mean molar mass Mw 70,000 and a calcium carbonate dispersing capacity of 280 mg/g active polymer (snippet: "at 60 … pH 11"). Its residue is "friable and thus will not promote re-soiling" **(attribution not pinned between BASF product pages)** — [BASF Care 360, Sokalan CP 5 Granules](https://care360.basf.com/global/en/Product-Finder/30042797)
- Sokalan CP 5 is used in laundry detergent as an anti-redeposition agent, encrustation inhibitor, viscosity modifier and process aid, and is "especially effective at sequestering calcium and magnesium ions" — [ChemPoint, Sokalan CP 5](https://www.chempoint.com/products/basf/sokalan-acrylic-polymers/sokalan-acrylic-polymers/sokalan-cp-5)
- BASF's technical information (dated January 1999) attributes incrustation inhibition by the CP types to "their capacity to disperse sparingly soluble solids" — [Sokalan CP types TI (Scribd copy)](https://www.scribd.com/document/518796468/Sokalan-CP-Types-TI-En)
- Sokalan PA 30 CL is a fully neutralised, low-molecular-weight polyacrylic acid supplied as a 45 % solids solution **(attribution not pinned)** — [ChemPoint Sokalan laundry selector](https://www.chempoint.com/guides/sokalan-acrylic-polymers-selector/sokalan-by-application/laundry-and-fabric-detergent/109122)
- Sodium polyacrylate stabilises PET-POET soil-release polymer during alkaline storage and is reported to increase its soil-release activity — [US 4,806,261](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4806261)
- Hydrophobically modified polycarboxylates do carry denim claims. US 6,337,313 covers a backbone of unsaturated C1–C6 acid with hydrophobic moieties from saturated or unsaturated alkyl, aryl and aryl-alkyl groups — [US 6,337,313](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6337313)
- CN103233377A describes a copolymer of acrylic acid, fumaric acid and N-vinylpyrrolidone esterified with cetyl alcohol — [CN103233377A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN103233377A/en)
- CN102392374B uses alkali-hydrolysed styrene/maleic anhydride copolymer — [CN102392374B](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN102392374B/en)
- A cotton-industry guide states that strongly ionic substances suppress cellulase while nonionic surfactants usually raise its activity — [Cotton Incorporated ISP-1003](https://www.cottoninc.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/ISP-1003-Enzyme-Technology-for-Cotton-Products.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE]** A plain polyacrylate has no hydrophobic or aromatic anchor for the indigo crystal face and is repelled by negatively charged cotton and polyester. It adsorbs well only on cationic or Ca-bearing mineral surfaces. Its expected contribution in a stonewash bath is to keep pumice fines and CaCO3 dispersed and to complex Ca2+/Mg2+ that would otherwise compress double layers and bridge negatively charged particles to the fibre.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Poly(acrylic acid) has an apparent pKa of about 4.5–6 that rises with degree of ionisation. Maleic units have pKa1 about 3–4 and pKa2 above 6. **[INFERENCE]** At the acid-cellulase pH of 4.5–5.5 only roughly a quarter to three quarters of acrylate groups are ionised, so charge density, electrostatic stabilisation and Ca binding are all clearly weaker than at pH 6–7 or in alkaline laundry (the CP 5 dispersing figure is quoted at pH 11). Polycarboxylates fit the neutral-cellulase window better. Sulfonate-bearing polymers keep full charge at pH 4.5.
- **[INFERENCE]** High-Mw CP 5 (70,000) is designed for encrustation control with zeolite/carbonate builders. For particle dispersion in a short-liquor denim bath, low-Mw homopolymer (about 2,000–8,000) is the logical grade because it gives more chains per gram and lower bridging risk.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Likely CAS numbers: sodium polyacrylate 9003-04-7; acrylic/maleic copolymer sodium salt 52255-49-9; styrene/maleic anhydride copolymer 9011-13-6.

### Gaps
- No study was found that tests unmodified polyacrylate or acrylic/maleic copolymer on indigo backstaining at pH 4.5–7.
- Mw of Sokalan PA 30 CL (commonly quoted as about 8,000) was not confirmed. Ca-binding capacities in mg CaCO3/g were not retrieved.
- No measured data on polycarboxylate ionisation or Ca binding at pH 4.5–5.5, and no data on polycarboxylate effects on cellulase activity or cellulose-binding-domain adsorption, were retrieved.

## 3. Polyester soil-release polymers (PET-POET block copolymers; sulfonated and non-ionic oligoesters)

### Takeaway
This is the only polymer class for which several independent patent families explicitly claim prevention of indigo redeposition in stonewashing, especially on non-cotton accessories such as pocket lining. The motif is a terephthalate polyester block that adsorbs onto hydrophobic polyester/polyamide, combined with polyether (PEG) blocks that form a hydrated film.

### Cited Findings
**Denim-specific patents**
- US 6,008,182 claims a process for preventing dye redeposition during washing of dyed fabric using a water-soluble or water-dispersible polyester resin composition. Its composition claims cover surfactant, enzyme, softener and 0.01–45 wt % polymer; dependent claims name indigo blue, vat dye, denim and prewashing/stonewashing — [US 6,008,182](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6008182A/en)
- Same patent, background: indigo blue is the most common dye released in stonewashing and it lands on white pockets and seams or back on the denim — [US 6,008,182](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6008182A/en)
- Koch, US 2008/0028539 A1 / EP 2009087 A2 (priority 10 Dec 2003): the redeposition inhibitor is a polyester made from dicarboxylic acids, C2–C6 diols and polyether alcohols having one or two OH groups and at least six oxygen atoms. These three monomer types make up more than 80 wt % of the polymer. The preferred type is a terephthalic acid polyester–polyether polymer — [US 2008/0028539 A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/US20080028539A1/en); [EP 2009087 A2](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2009087A2/en)
- Koch: the inhibitors show "affinity for hydrophobic surfaces, such as polyester, polyamide, and mixtures thereof with cotton". The target is denim accessories "such as pocket lining, stitching, labels and zippers, which are often not made of cotton". Claims cover indigo and stonewashing or biostoning of indigo-dyed cotton — [EP 2009087 A2](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2009087A2/en)
- US 10,435,647 B2 (The Seydel Companies): water-soluble polyesters made from bio-renewable raw materials for preventing dye redeposition in textile finishing and garment washing — [US 10,435,647 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10435647B2/en)
- CN102493234B: a denim-wash anti-staining agent that is a water-soluble polyester made by esterifying a polyether dicarboxylic acid with ethylene glycol at 1:1 molar ratio; a white waxy water-soluble solid. The patent argues ionic surfactants can bind proteins and inactivate cellulase — [CN102493234B](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN102493234B/en)

**Structure and molecular weight (laundry patents)**
- PET-POET is a copolymer of ethylene terephthalate and polyoxyethylene terephthalate units with a preferred molar ratio of about 25:75 to 35:65. Its molecular weight is about 500–120,000, with 2,000–25,000 preferred — [US 6,242,404](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6242404B1/en)
- Polyoxyethylene blocks are typically 1,000–10,000 in molecular weight, and the ethylene terephthalate : POET ratio is often cited as 2:1 to 6:1 — [US 4,785,060](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4785060A/en); [US 5,840,323](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5840323)
- Other stated ranges: about 5,000–50,000 with 19,000–43,000 typical — [US 5,866,525](https://patents.google.com/patent/US5866525A/en). Best performance at weight-average Mw about 15,000–35,000 — [US 4,785,060](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4785060A/en)

**Commercial product statements**
- Clariant TexCare SRN 170 SG Terra is a "nonionic soil release polyester". On washing "the polymer is adsorbed onto the hydrophobic fibres and forms a hydrophilic film", and its amphiphilic structure helps disperse soil. It is derived from plant-based polyalkylene glycol; Clariant pages give its Renewable Carbon Index as 65 % in one place and about 75 % in another — [Clariant TexCare SRN 170 SG Terra](https://www.clariant.com/en/Products/TexCare-SRN-170-SG-Terra)
- TexCare One Terra is listed as a water-soluble polyester — [Clariant TexCare One Terra](https://www.clariant.com/en/Products/TexCare-One-Terra)

**Why polyester is the critical surface**
- Reported fabric zeta potentials: polyester about −69.0 mV, cotton −24.5 mV, viscose −20.6 mV; fabric zeta potential becomes more negative as pH rises **(attribution not pinned between two sources)** — [Colloids Surf. A study on pigment inks](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S092777572202444X); [Electrokinetic Properties of Textile Fabrics](https://www.academia.edu/11434006/Electrokinetic_Properties_of_Textile_Fabrics)
- Boardman et al. (J. Appl. Polym. Sci. 2020) measured indigo transfer onto several fibre types and reported an 82 % reduction in colour change on polyester for one PEG-containing condensation polymer — [Boardman et al. 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/app.49632)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE]** Mechanism on the polyester pocket: the oligo(alkylene terephthalate) block is chemically identical to the PET surface and adsorbs by hydrophobic, π–π and dipolar ester–ester interaction. The PEG blocks cannot enter the fibre and form a hydrated steric layer. Polyester is strongly negative yet still stains heavily with indigo, so charge repulsion alone does not protect it; the staining is driven by hydrophobic/dispersion attraction of the non-ionic planar indigo, as with a disperse dye. Removing the hydrophobic interface is what works.
- **[INFERENCE]** The same amphiphile should adsorb on the hydrophobic indigo particle (aromatic ester anchor, PEG buoy) and act as a steric dispersant in the liquor. That would explain why these polyesters are claimed for redeposition generally and not only for polyester parts. No adsorption data on indigo were found to confirm it.
- **[INFERENCE]** Arithmetic from the patent ranges: PEG blocks of 1,000–10,000 correspond to about 23–227 EO units. Koch's "at least six oxygen atoms" means polyether alcohols from about 5 EO upward, including mono-OH (methyl-capped) types that act as chain terminators and give low-Mw comb-like or end-capped oligoesters.
- **[INFERENCE]** The two PET:POET ratios quoted (25:75–35:65 versus 2:1–6:1) are not consistent. They probably reflect different definitions (molar ratio of units versus a different counting basis) or different product generations. Check the originals before quoting.
- **[INFERENCE]** Non-ionic versus anionic variants in a denim bath. Non-ionic (methyl-PEG-capped) oligoesters are pH-independent, compatible with cellulase and adsorb most strongly on polyester. Sulfonated variants add water solubility and electrostatic stabilisation of dispersed indigo, and sulfonate stays fully ionised at pH 4.5, unlike carboxylate; the cost is some repulsion from the strongly negative polyester surface. Ester hydrolysis is slow at pH 4.5–7 and 40–60 °C, far milder than alkaline laundry where these polymers need stabilisation.
- **[INFERENCE]** These polymers have no specific affinity for cellulose, so they do not by themselves block the cellulase-protein-mediated route of indigo onto cotton (section 9). A complete agent needs a second function for cotton.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Product-family descriptions recalled, not verified: TexCare SRN 100/170/240/300 as non-ionic terephthalate/propylene glycol/methyl-PEG oligoesters; TexCare SRA 100/300 as anionic 5-sulfoisophthalate-containing polyesters; Repel-O-Tex SRP 6 / Crystal / SF 2 as PEG–terephthalate polyesters; Marloquest as Sasol's soil-release polyester range; P&G/Gosselink sulfo-end-capped oligoester patents (for example US 4,702,857, US 4,721,580, US 4,877,896, US 4,968,451, US 5,415,807). CAS 9016-88-0 is commonly used for PET-POET copolymer.

### Gaps

## 4. Cellulose derivatives (Na-CMC), modified starches, PVA

### Takeaway
CMC is the historical cotton anti-redeposition agent (detergent grades with DS about 0.45–0.9) because it adsorbs specifically onto cellulose, which is also why it does not protect synthetics. No denim-wash evidence was found for it, and by inference it is a poor fit for a cellulase bath because it is itself a cellulase substrate.

### Cited Findings
- CMC was identified in the 1930s as a suitable agent against soil redeposition (greying) and has been the standard since **(attribution not pinned)** — [PMC8038726](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8038726)
- Detergent-grade CMC is usually reported at a degree of substitution of roughly 0.6–0.9 **(attribution not pinned)** — [PMC8038726](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8038726)
- A laundry patent states that sodium CMC with DS of about 0.5 and above dissolves and works as an anti-redeposition agent, whereas DS 0.2 does not dissolve in water — [US 8,349,789](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8349789)
- A supplier anti-redeposition grade (CMC FD40) lists DS 0.45–0.55 — [Kima CMC FD40](https://www.kimacellulose.com/products/carboxymethyl-cellulose-cmc-fd40/)
- A study of CMC on cotton describes a specific adsorption on cellulose governed by the polymer, the cotton and a third factor — [Sen'i Gakkaishi 28(9), 374](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/fiber1944/28/9/28_9_374/_article/-char/en)
- EP 0634486 A1 claims acrylamide polymers (50–100 wt % acrylamide or N-substituted acrylamide, 0–50 wt % vinyl monomer) for preventing dye deposition in fabric finishing, and also mentions acrylic carboxylate emulsion polymers and polyethoxylated urethane thickeners — [EP 0634486 A1](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP0634486A1/en)
- Another patent in the same family area describes poly(amino acids) containing carboxylic acid groups as useful for preventing dye deposition **(attribution not pinned; probably US 5,380,447)** — [US 5,380,447](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5380447)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE]** Why cotton and not synthetics: the CMC backbone is a β-1,4-glucan identical to cellulose, so unsubstituted segments (favoured at low DS, 0.4–0.7) can lie on and H-bond with the cotton surface while carboxymethyl groups add negative charge and hydration. Polyester offers no such recognition and is already strongly negative, so the polyanion does not adsorb. The cited "specific adsorption on cellulose" supports this; the H-bond/co-crystallisation detail is my reasoning.
- **[INFERENCE — important for denim]** Soluble CMC is the standard assay substrate for endoglucanase ("CMCase" activity) [BACKGROUND – unverified]. In a cellulase bath CMC would be degraded, losing its molecular weight and function, and would compete with the fabric surface for the enzyme, altering abrasion. CMC also works by sitting on cotton, which is the surface the enzyme must reach. CMC is therefore unsuited to the enzyme step and at most usable in a later enzyme-free rinse.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** The carboxymethyl pKa is about 4.3, so CMC is only partly ionised at pH 4.5–5 and loses charge and solubility below about pH 4. Sodium CMC CAS is 9004-32-4.
- **[INFERENCE]** Starch derivatives are not cellulase substrates, but denim baths often carry residual α-amylase from desizing, which would degrade them. Cationic starches would deposit on negatively charged fibres with any indigo they bind. Partially hydrolysed PVA (CAS 9002-89-5 [BACKGROUND – unverified]) is a mild amphiphilic protective colloid whose residual acetate groups provide anchoring, but no indigo data were found.
- **[INFERENCE]** The acrylamide and poly(amino acid) claims show that amide-rich, H-bonding, non-ionic or weakly anionic water-soluble polymers were considered effective against dye deposition in finishing baths. This fits the idea that amide/lactam C=O groups interact with indigo N–H at the particle surface.

### Gaps
- No data on CMC (DS, Mw) performance against indigo backstaining and no study quantifying CMC degradation or competition in an acid or neutral cellulase denim bath were retrieved.
- Typical detergent-CMC molecular weights were not retrieved.
- No evidence was found for modified starches or PVA as indigo anti-backstaining agents.
- Examples and performance data of EP 0634486 A1 and US 5,380,447 were not retrieved.

## 5. Amphiphilic graft, block and comb copolymers (PEG-g-PVAc, EO/PO blocks, alkoxylated PEI, hydrophobically modified polymers, SMA, comb polymers)

### Takeaway
The patent and journal evidence most specific to indigo points to amphiphilic "anchor–buoy" polymers: hydrophobic or aromatic segments (vinyl acetate, styrene, aryl-alkyl, cetyl ester, terephthalate) joined to hydrophilic segments (PEG, carboxylate, amide/lactam). One peer-reviewed study reports an 82 % reduction in indigo colour change on polyester at 0.1 mg/mL for a PEG-containing condensation polymer.

### Cited Findings
**PEG-graft-poly(vinyl acetate), Sokalan HP 22**

- Such graft copolymers use a water-soluble polyalkylene oxide graft base with side chains from polymerised vinyl ester, with typical Mw of about 3,000–100,000 **(attribution not pinned)** — [US 8,334,250](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/8334250)
- A patent describes the commercial product as a 22 % aqueous solution of poly(ethylene oxide-g-vinyl acetate) **(attribution not pinned)** — [US 10,538,631](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/10538631)

**Alkoxylated polyethyleneimine**
- BASF describes Sokalan HP 20 as a multifunctional modified polyethylene imine in aqueous solution at about 80 % concentration, classed under dispersing ingredients — [BASF Sokalan HP 20](https://www.homecare-and-i-and-i.basf.com/products/products-detail/Sokalan%20HP%2020/30516313)

Use level is 0.001–50 wt %, narrowed to 0.1–1 wt % in dependent claims — [US 6,537,327](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6537327)

**PEG-containing condensation polymers tested on indigo**
- Boardman, Hayward, Lant, Fossum & Thornton (J. Appl. Polym. Sci. 2020, 138, e49632): DTIs made by condensation reactions were effective against unbound indigo on several fibre types. Several showed potential "at very low concentrations (0.1 mg/ml)". DTI9, which incorporates diethanolamine, gave an 82 % reduction in colour change onto polyester caused by indigo. Tris(hydroxymethyl)aminomethane-containing polymers were also successful, and a pentaerythritol-containing polymer was compared in mPEG500 and mPEG750 variants — [Boardman et al. 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/app.49632); [White Rose record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/162910/)

**General dispersant-design literature**
- Polymeric dispersants combine an anchoring part that binds the pigment with a solvated chain that gives steric repulsion. - Creutz & Jérôme (J. - For C.I. Pigment Violet 23, longer PEG side chains gave smaller particles and stronger steric stabilisation — [Colloid Polym. Sci. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00396-021-04937-z)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE]** Sokalan HP 22 arithmetic: PEG 6000 (136 EO) plus three times its weight of vinyl acetate gives 6,000 + 18,000 = 24,000, matching the patent figure. That is about 209 vinyl acetate units (86.09 g/mol) per chain and an EO weight fraction of about 25 %, a Griffin-equivalent HLB of about 5. It is a strongly hydrophobic, dispersible amphiphile. The PVAc grafts are the anchor for hydrophobic surfaces (polyester, and presumably indigo) and the PEG backbone is the buoy. Being non-ionic, it should be pH-independent over 4.5–7 and inert towards cellulase. No indigo data were found.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Later P&G patents describe the same product type as PEG 6000 backbone with PEG:PVAc of about 40:60 by weight and no more than one graft point per 50 EO units. Probable CAS is 25820-49-9.
- **[INFERENCE]** Common pattern across the denim-claimed polymers: the hydrophobes are aromatic or long-alkyl (styrene, aryl-alkyl, cetyl, terephthalate, vinyl acetate) and the hydrophilic parts are carboxylate, PEG or amide/lactam. No denim-claimed polymer found here is cationic.
- **[INFERENCE]** Boardman's active polymers are PEG-rich, non-ionic to weakly basic, with short mPEG chains (500–750 Da, about 11–17 EO) and polyol or amino-alcohol cores, and they work at 0.1 g/L. This suggests long PEG chains are not required and that multi-hydroxyl/amine H-bonding cores help. The mechanism (indigo particle stabilisation versus fibre-surface blocking) was not available.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** EO/PO triblocks (poloxamer, CAS 9003-11-6) adsorb through the PPO mid-block with PEO tails as stabilisers. Examples from memory: F68 about 8,400 g/mol and 80 % EO; F127 about 12,600 g/mol and 70 % EO; L64 about 2,900 g/mol and 40 % EO with a cloud point near 58 °C. They are low-foaming. **[INFERENCE]** PPO has no aromatic or H-bonding affinity for indigo, so anchoring should be weaker than with aromatic anchors; their main value is low-foam wetting.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Ethoxylated PEI in detergent patents is typically a PEI 600 core with about 20 EO per N–H. **[INFERENCE]** At pH 4.5–5.5 the core tertiary amines would be partly protonated, giving a weakly cationic core inside a PEG shell. That helps on negatively charged clay, but in a denim bath it risks substantivity to cotton and polyester and complexation with anionic co-dispersants, so it is a higher-risk choice at acid pH than a fully non-ionic amphiphile.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Polycarboxylate-ether combs (methacrylic acid-co-mPEG methacrylate) and styrene–acrylic or SMA resins are standard aqueous organic-pigment dispersants in inks. **[INFERENCE]** SMA's phenyl rings can stack on the planar indigo face while carboxylates provide charge, consistent with its use in CN102392374B.

## 6. Non-ionic surfactants (alcohol ethoxylates, castor oil / tristyrylphenol / alkylphenol / fatty amine ethoxylates, APG)

### Takeaway
Ethoxylated non-ionic surfactants are the best-documented small-molecule tool: a peer-reviewed study reports more than 75 % reduction of indigo adsorption at below 0.2 g/L, with cellulase action enhanced, whereas ionic surfactants depress cellulase. Patents nevertheless judge surfactants alone insufficient and combine them with polymers and fatty amine ethoxylates.

### Cited Findings
- Zilz et al. (Coloration Technology 2013; Hypocrea jecorina cellulases): "Indigo adsorption was successfully reduced by more than 75% with ethoxylated nonionic surfactants at concentrations below 0.2 g l−1" — [Zilz et al. 2013](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cote.12003/pdf); [CORE copy](https://core.ac.uk/reader/55625270)
- Same study: non-ionic surfactants and PVP significantly increased cotton weight loss after 120 min; protein adsorption on cotton fell with rising additive concentration, non-ionics being more efficient than PVP; a complete cellulase mixture and an endoglucanase-rich, exoglucanase-free preparation responded differently — [Zilz et al. 2013](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cote.12003/pdf)
- The same paper reports that ionic surfactants can denature and inactivate the enzyme while small amounts of non-ionic surfactants improve its action — [ResearchGate record](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/264452207_Nonionic_surfactants_and_dispersants_for_biopolishing_and_stonewashing_with_Hypocrea_jecorina_cellulases)
- A review of cellulases on denim reports that ionic surfactants lowered enzymatic hydrolysis and non-ionic ones increased it — [Use of cellulases and their effects on denim fabric properties](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/281258031_Use_of_cellulases_and_their_effects_on_denim_fabric_properties)
- Cotton Incorporated: strongly ionic substances suppress cellulase, non-ionic surfactants usually raise its activity; listed backstaining controls are a modified-acid or neutral cellulase, anti-redeposition agents and smaller loads — [Cotton Incorporated ISP-1003](https://www.cottoninc.com/wp-content/uploads/2017/12/ISP-1003-Enzyme-Technology-for-Cotton-Products.pdf)
- Iogen patents: adding a surfactant during the cellulase wash "further reduces backstaining without significant loss of net dye release"; extra anti-redeposition surfactants help "to some limited degree" but "some objectionable backstaining remains" — [US 5,700,686](https://patents.justia.com/patent/5700686); [US 5,916,799](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5916799)
- US 6,251,144 names the polyoxyethylated alcohol Triton series as preferred non-ionic surfactants for stonewashing with redepositing cellulase — [US 6,251,144](https://patents.justia.com/patent/6251144)
- An early stonewash-cellulase patent describes aliphatic alcohol alkoxylates as typical non-ionics that do not degrade enzyme activity — [US 4,912,056](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/4912056)
- CN103233377A: non-ionic surfactants interfere less with cellulase but are less effective at stopping redeposition, so polyoxyethylene ethers are combined with a fatty amine polyoxyethylene ether (5–20 % of the agent) and a hydrophobically modified polymer — [CN103233377A](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN103233377A/en)
- CN102392374B specifies fatty amine polyoxyethylene ether 2–10 % and fatty alcohol polyoxyethylene ether 5–25 % — [CN102392374B](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN102392374B/en)
- A pigment study with polyoxyethylene lauryl ether as sole dispersant obtained particle sizes of 150–200 nm and zeta potentials of −24 to −32 mV (pigment identity not confirmed as indigo) — [Agbo, Pigment & Resin Technology 2019](https://emerald.com/insight/content/doi/10.1108/PRT-10-2017-0081/full/html)
- Restrictions: the ZDHC MRSL entry for nonylphenol ethoxylates reads "no intentional use" with a formulation limit of 500 ppm (page is from the MRSL 2.0 guidance; not confirmed for v3.1) — [ZDHC MRSL guidance](https://mrsl.roadmaptozero.com/MRSL2_0?guidance=1)
- A brand restricted-substances document states NPEO is restricted in washable textile products at 100 mg/kg **(secondary source; attribution not pinned)** — [H&M Group chemical restrictions 2025](https://hmgroup.com/wp-content/uploads/2025/01/HM-Group-chemical-restrictions-textile-products-accessories-footwear-bags-and-belts-2025.pdf)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE — own Griffin calculations, HLB = 20 × M(EO)/M(total)]**

| Surfactant (assumed hydrophobe mass) | EO units | Calculated HLB |
|---|---|---|
| Isotridecanol, C13H27OH (200.4) | 6 / 8 / 10 / 12 | 11.4 / 12.7 / 13.7 / 14.5 |
| C12–14 fatty alcohol (≈194) | 7 | 12.3 |
| C16–18 fatty alcohol (≈256) | 25 | 16.2 |
| Castor oil (≈933) | 36 / 40 | 12.6 / 13.1 |
| Tristyrylphenol, C30H30O (406.6) | 16 / 20 / 29 | 12.7 / 13.7 / 15.2 |
| Nonylphenol (220.4), restricted | 9 / 10 | 12.9 / 13.3 |

- **[INFERENCE]** No source found here states an optimum HLB, EO number or cloud point for indigo. From general pigment-wetting practice [BACKGROUND – unverified], the useful range is HLB about 12–16. The lower end (iso-C13 with 7–9 EO, HLB 12–13) wets and deaggregates; the upper end (C16–18 with 20–25 EO; tristyrylphenol with 16–29 EO; castor oil with 36–40 EO) gives longer EO chains for steric stabilisation and a cloud point well above the 40–60 °C bath. A surfactant used above its cloud point phase-separates and can carry indigo onto fibres, so the cloud point should exceed bath temperature by a clear margin.
- **[INFERENCE]** Why ethoxylates work beyond micellar solubilisation. Zilz shows they reduce cellulase-protein adsorption on cotton while raising activity. Cellulase protein binding to cotton is reported as the main backstaining cause (section 9), so ethoxylates act partly by blocking or lubricating protein–cellulose and protein–indigo association. This second mechanism is absent in ordinary laundry DTI thinking.
- **[INFERENCE]** Tristyrylphenol ethoxylates carry three pendant phenyl rings, the same π-anchor idea as NSF condensates and SMA but in a non-ionic, cellulase-friendly molecule. They are logical APEO-free successors to NPEO for planar aromatic pigments. No indigo data were found.
- **[INFERENCE]** Fatty amine ethoxylates are partly protonated at pH 4.5–5.5 and therefore weakly cationic, giving affinity for dye and fibre. The Chinese patents cap them at 2–20 % of the agent, consistent with use as a minority co-surfactant; at high levels the cationic character would promote deposition on negative fibres.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Alkyl polyglucosides are non-ionic, readily biodegradable and electrolyte-tolerant with no cloud point, but they foam strongly and their glucoside head offers no particular affinity for indigo. Guerbet (2-propylheptanol, C10) ethoxylates are low-foam fast wetters with short hydrophobes, hence weaker anchoring.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** REACH Annex XVII entry 46 limits nonylphenol and its ethoxylates to 0.1 % in textile-processing formulations, and entry 46a limits NPEO to below 0.01 % by weight in washable textile articles (applicable from February 2021), which matches the 100 mg/kg figure. Likely CAS numbers: isotridecanol ethoxylate 69011-36-5 / 9043-30-5; C12–14 alcohol ethoxylate 68439-50-9; castor oil ethoxylate 61791-12-6; tristyrylphenol ethoxylate 99734-09-5; NPEO 9016-45-9; tallow amine ethoxylate 61791-26-2; APG 68515-73-1 (C8–10) and 110615-47-9 (C10–16).

### Gaps
- Zilz et al. full text not read: the identity (alkyl chain, EO number, HLB) of the ethoxylates behind the ">75 %" result, the test substrate and the pH/temperature are unknown.
- No published comparison of HLB, EO number or cloud point against indigo dispersion or backstaining was found, and none for castor oil ethoxylates, tristyrylphenol ethoxylates, Guerbet ethoxylates or APG with indigo.
- Supplier cloud-point data (for example Lutensol TO, Marlipal O13) were not retrieved.

- No data on surfactant or polymer compatibility with laccase/mediator bleaching systems were retrieved.

## 7. Anionic dispersants for vat/disperse dyes (naphthalene sulfonate–formaldehyde condensates, lignosulfonates, phenol sulfonate condensates, polystyrene sulfonate)

### Takeaway
Naphthalene sulfonate–formaldehyde condensates and lignosulfonates are the established dispersants for vat and disperse dye preparations, so they are chemically matched to indigo as a pigment. No source found here evaluates them as denim anti-backstaining agents, and their coloured, fibre-substantive and strongly anionic nature is a known drawback class.

### Cited Findings
- Naphthalenesulfonic acid–formaldehyde polymer sodium salt, CAS 36290-04-7 ("Dispersant NNO"), is "mainly used as a dispersant in disperse dyes and vat dyes" — [ChemicalBook CAS 36290-04-7](https://www.chemicalbook.com/CASEN_36290-04-7.htm)
- Its formula is written as the repeating unit (C10H8O3S·CH2O)x·xNa; suppliers quote inconsistent single "molecular weights" (238, 260, 472) that correspond to monomer or dimer units, not a polymer average — [Parchem](https://parchem.com/chemical-supplier-distributor/2-naphthalenesulfonic-acid-polymer-with-formaldehyde-sodium-salt-040437)
- Dye dispersants are "generally one of two major types, sulfonated lignins from the wood pulping industry or naphthalene sulfonates from the petroleum industry"; "the disadvantages of naphthalene sulfonates include heat stability and little dye retarding properties", whereas lignin dispersants also act as dye retarders — [US 4,131,564](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4131564)
- Dispersible vat dye powders have long been made by pasting finely divided insoluble colour with partially desulfonated lignosulfonate and water, then drying — [US 2,685,494](https://patents.google.com/patent/US2685494)
- General colloid guidance: zeta potentials beyond ±30 mV indicate good electrostatic stability, but steric and hydrophobic effects also matter — [Microtrac](https://microtrac.com/knowledge/stability-of-colloidal-dispersion)

### Inferences
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Structure: β-naphthalenesulfonic acid units joined by methylene bridges, typically 2–10 or more units, with one –SO3Na per ring. Sulfonic acid is fully ionised at every wash pH. A second CAS in use is 9084-06-4. Trade names recalled: Tamol NN, Setamol WS, Dispersogen.
- **[INFERENCE]** Mechanism on indigo: the fused naphthalene rings lie flat on the planar indigo crystal face (π–π and hydrophobic interaction) and the sulfonates point into the water, giving a strongly negative particle. The key advantage over polycarboxylates is full charge at pH 4.5–5.5. The main limitation is that stabilisation is purely electrostatic, so it is weakened by the high ionic strength of a buffered, hard-water, short-liquor bath; non-ionic PEG buoys are less sensitive.
- **[INFERENCE — reasons for caution in a denim bath]** (a) These condensates are brown and substantive to polyamide and wool [BACKGROUND – unverified: related condensates are used as syntans and nylon reserving agents], so they risk staining or yellowing elastane/nylon components and white pocketing. (b) They are polyanionic and aromatic, and the cited general rule is that strongly ionic substances suppress cellulase. (c) They are poorly biodegradable and may carry residual formaldehyde or naphthalene, which is relevant to MRSL/eco-label screening [BACKGROUND – unverified]. (d) Lignosulfonates are darker and, per the cited patent, act as dye retarders, meaning they bind dye strongly; that is useful for holding indigo but increases the staining risk if the dispersant itself deposits.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Sodium lignosulfonate (CAS 8061-51-6) has a broad molecular-weight distribution from about 1,000 to above 50,000. Sodium polystyrene sulfonate (CAS 25704-18-1, typical Mw about 70,000) is a colourless strong polyelectrolyte with pendant phenyl rings and is the logical non-staining analogue. Phenol-sulfonic acid/formaldehyde and ditolyl-ether sulfonate condensates are the lighter-coloured syntan-type relatives.
- **[INFERENCE]** Indigo already in commerce (powder, granules, paste) is formulated with this dispersant class, so some of it enters the wash bath with the dye. A wash-bath agent should therefore be compatible with, not precipitated by, strong polyanions; this is another argument against cationic agents.

### Gaps
- No published adsorption isotherm, zeta-potential or dispersion-stability study of indigo with NSF condensates, lignosulfonates or polystyrene sulfonate was found; the one dedicated search returned only generic material.
- Degree of condensation and Mw for Tamol NN, Setamol WS and Dispersogen grades were not retrieved.
- No documented evidence was retrieved on fibre staining or yellowing by NSF or lignosulfonate under denim-wash conditions, or on their effect on cellulase activity. These remain inferences.

## 8. Other approaches: cyclodextrins, proteins and enzymes, scavengers, clays, cationic versus amphoteric polymers

### Takeaway
Cyclodextrin inclusion of indigo is now documented (1:1 complexes with β-CD and HP-β-CD, 2026) but is stoichiometric and so mass-inefficient at denim-bath indigo loads. Protein-level interventions (protease treatment, lipolytic enzymes, cellulases without cellulose-binding domains) are well documented because the cellulase protein is itself an indigo carrier. No sources were found for polyamide powder or clay scavengers with indigo.

### Cited Findings
- Devi, Krishnan, Yeo & Choi (Bioresources and Bioprocessing 2026, 13:71): 1:1 molar inclusion complexes of indigo with β-cyclodextrin and hydroxypropyl-β-cyclodextrin were prepared to enhance solubility and stability without chemical intervention, and the approach was extended to natural Jeju indigo — [Devi et al. 2026](https://journal.hep.com.cn/bab/EN/10.1186/s40643-026-01065-w)
- Indigo carmine with cyclodextrins (Applied Spectroscopy 1998): induced circular dichroism in 2-hydroxypropyl-β-CD indicates axial inclusion along the CD symmetry axis; binding was studied at pH 7 and pH 12, 25 and 37 °C — [Roberts, Burguieres & Warner 1998](https://repository.lsu.edu/chemistry_pubs/2690)
- A β-CD inclusion complex of another vat dye (Vat Red 13) cut the reducing agent needed by about 75 % in vat dyeing of viscose — [PMC11920092](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC11920092/)
- Native β-cyclodextrin dissolves only to about 18.5 mg/mL in water at room temperature **(attribution not pinned)** — [PMC3759929](https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3759929/)
- Protease: adding protease during or after the cellulase step reduces backstaining, but it is costly and needs careful control because proteases tend to destroy cellulase — [US 5,700,686](https://patents.justia.com/patent/5700686); [US 5,916,799](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/5916799)

- Koch's polyester patents state that polyamide, like polyester, is a hydrophobic surface with affinity relevant to indigo redeposition — [EP 2009087 A2](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2009087A2/en)
- Fabric surfaces are negatively charged (polyester about −69 mV, cotton about −24.5 mV) **(attribution not pinned)** — [Colloids Surf. A study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S092777572202444X)

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE — own calculation]** β-CD is 1,134.98 g/mol and indigo 262.27 g/mol, so a 1:1 complex needs 4.3 g β-CD per gram of indigo. With roughly 0.4–0.8 g/L indigo in the bath (illustrative estimate, section 9) that is about 1.7–3.5 g/L β-CD at 100 % complexation. This is within the solubility of native β-CD (about 18.5 g/L) but is several times the mass of the dye, whereas a polymeric dispersant works by surface adsorption at a fraction of the pigment mass. HP-β-CD raises solubility further but does not change the stoichiometry. Indigo is also crystalline, and extracting molecules from the lattice into the cavity must overcome the H-bonded, π-stacked lattice energy. Cyclodextrin is a scientifically valid but economically weak route for bulk backstaining control.
- **[INFERENCE]** Because the cellulase protein (through its binding domain) carries indigo to cotton, "sacrificial" amphiphiles that occupy the hydrophobic binding patches on protein and indigo (ethoxylates, and possibly non-catalytic proteins or hydrolysates) are mechanistically reasonable. Only the surfactant version is documented here.
- **[INFERENCE]** Polyamide powder as a sacrificial scavenger is plausible, since indigo has affinity for polyamide surfaces and nylon offers amide N–H/C=O for H-bonding, but no source was found. A particulate scavenger must also be separable from garments, and pumice already provides a large competing surface.
- **[INFERENCE]** Bentonite, laponite and zeolite are negatively charged and adsorb cationic species best. Indigo is non-ionic, so only weak adsorption is expected; clays may instead adsorb cellulase protein and reduce activity. No data were found.
- **[INFERENCE — firm design rule]** Cationic polymers (polyDADMAC, polyamines, cationic "dye-catcher" chemistries) adsorb strongly to negatively charged cotton and polyester, would flocculate anionically dispersed indigo and would then fix the floc to the fabric. They suit a separate dye-catcher substrate, not a bath additive. Amphoteric or betaine polymers avoid net cationic charge above about pH 3–4 [BACKGROUND – unverified] and are acceptable.

### Gaps
- Quantitative results of the 2026 indigo–cyclodextrin paper (binding constant, solubility gain, stability) were not retrieved, and neither were the 1998 indigo carmine binding constants.
- No sources were found on polyamide/nylon powder scavengers, bentonite/laponite/zeolite or protein hydrolysates for indigo backstaining.
- No data were found on amphoteric polymers other than poly(vinylpyridine betaine) in denim washing.

## 9. Indigo itself: molecular properties governing interaction; published indigo dispersion, adsorption and backstaining studies

### Takeaway
Indigo (C16H10N2O2, 262.27 g/mol) is a planar, non-ionic molecule whose N–H and C=O groups are tied up in intra- and intermolecular hydrogen bonds and whose molecules stack at about 3.4 Å, giving very low water solubility; in the wash bath it is a hydrophobic particle, not a dissolved dye. The best-documented backstaining driver is the cellulase protein, which binds indigo and cotton and reduces indigo particle size, and backstaining is worse at acid pH.

### Inferences
- **[INFERENCE — functional-group affinity ranking for the indigo particle surface, by reasoning, not by measurement]**
  1. Planar aromatic or conjugated groups able to stack face-to-face on the indigo π-face at about 3.4 Å: naphthalene sulfonate, styryl/phenyl, terephthalate ester, pyridine N-oxide. Strongest and least water-sensitive, because dispersion forces and the hydrophobic effect add up over the whole 16-carbon chromophore.
  2. Strong H-bond acceptors that can engage surface N–H groups at crystal edges: N-oxide, then lactam and tertiary amide C=O, then ester C=O, then ether oxygen. Only edge or defect N–H is available, since each N–H is already used intra- or intermolecularly, so this is a secondary contribution in water.
  3. H-bond donors (OH, NH) for indigo C=O: weak in water because of competition with solvent.
  4. Aliphatic hydrophobes (C12–C18 alkyl, PPO, PVAc): non-specific hydrophobic adsorption; useful anchors but weaker per unit area than aromatic stacking.
  5. Ethoxy chains: little affinity for indigo itself; their role is the solvated buoy, with some affinity for protein surfaces.
- **[INFERENCE]** Indigo has no ionisable group in the pH 4.5–7 range, so pH acts indirectly: through the charge of fibre and dispersant, through cellulase binding, and through the cellulase-induced reduction in indigo particle size. The observation that backstaining is worse at acid pH fits lower fibre zeta potential, lower carboxylate dispersant charge and stronger binding of acid cellulase all at once.
- **[INFERENCE — illustrative bath load; assumptions are mine]** If denim carries about 1–3 % indigo on fabric weight and 10–30 % of it is released, 1 kg of fabric at 1:5–1:10 liquor ratio gives roughly 0.1–1.8 g/L, centred near 0.4–0.8 g/L, of suspended indigo. That is far above the ppm-level regime of household laundry, where the PVNO patents test the polymer at only 7–10 ppm in the liquor. It is a plausible reason why ppm-level complexants fail and why g/L-level dispersants and surface blockers are needed.
- **[BACKGROUND – unverified]** Indigo CAS 482-89-3; trans-planar with two intramolecular N–H···O=C bonds, each molecule also H-bonded to four neighbours; melting/sublimation near 390 °C; N–H pKa above 12. Indigo carmine (5,5′-disulfonate, CAS 860-22-0) is water-soluble and anionic, so it models the chromophore's H-bonding and π-stacking but not the particulate, non-ionic behaviour of indigo in a wash bath. Bare organic pigment particles in water usually carry a moderately negative zeta potential.

## 10. Design rules: what the structure and formula of an ideal denim anti-backstaining agent should look like

### Takeaway
The converging evidence favours a non-ionic (or sulfonate-anionic) amphiphilic anchor–buoy oligomer or polymer of roughly 3,000–30,000 g/mol, with aromatic ester or aryl anchors and PEG buoys, used together with an ethoxylated non-ionic surfactant; cationic character should be avoided. This recommendation is a synthesis by inference. No single source states it and no head-to-head data set was retrieved.

### Cited Findings
- Hydrophobicity must be addressed: PVP and PVNO are stated to fail on indigo "due to the extreme hydrophobicity of indigo dyes", and hydrophobically modified polymers are claimed instead at 0.1–1 wt % — [US 6,537,327](https://image-ppubs.uspto.gov/dirsearch-public/print/downloadPdf/6537327)
- Terephthalate polyester–polyether polymers with affinity for hydrophobic surfaces are claimed against indigo redeposition, especially on non-cotton accessories — [EP 2009087 A2](https://patents.google.com/patent/EP2009087A2/en); [US 6,008,182](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6008182A/en); [US 10,435,647 B2](https://patents.google.com/patent/US10435647B2/en)
- PEG-containing condensation polymers at 0.1 mg/mL gave up to 82 % reduction of indigo colour change on polyester — [Boardman et al. 2020](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/app.49632)
- Ethoxylated non-ionics below 0.2 g/L reduced indigo adsorption by more than 75 % and increased cellulase action; ionic surfactants can denature cellulase — [Zilz et al. 2013](http://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/cote.12003/pdf)
- Cellulase protein binding to cotton is the major cause of backstaining, and backstaining is worse at acid pH — [Cavaco-Paulo et al. 1998](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/249784914_Indigo_Backstaining_During_Cellulase_Washing); [Indigo–Cellulase Interactions](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/220030789_Indigo-Cellulase_Interactions)
- For DTI polymers, molecular weight gains level off at about 20,000 — [Oakes & Dixon 2003](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1478-4408.2003.tb00164.x); soil-release polyesters perform best at Mw about 15,000–35,000 or are preferred at 2,000–25,000 — [US 4,785,060](https://patents.google.com/patent/US4785060A/en); [US 6,242,404](https://patents.google.com/patent/US6242404B1/en)
- Dispersant architecture: an anchoring segment plus solvated stabilising chains; comb and block designs with multiple anchors; electrosteric designs tolerate higher ionic strength; longer PEG side chains strengthen steric stabilisation of an organic pigment — [Creutz & Jérôme 1998](https://www.paint.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/jctAUG98-Creutz.pdf); [Colloid Polym. Sci. 2021](https://link.springer.com/10.1007/s00396-021-04937-z)
- Practical formulations combine functions: SMA hydrolysate, PVP, fatty amine ethoxylate and fatty alcohol ethoxylate in one product — [CN102392374B](https://patents.google.com/patent/CN102392374B/en)
- Fibre surfaces are negative (polyester about −69 mV, cotton about −24.5 mV) **(attribution not pinned)** — [Colloids Surf. A study](https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S092777572202444X)
- APEO chemistry is excluded: NPEO "no intentional use", 500 ppm formulation limit (MRSL 2.0 page) — [ZDHC MRSL guidance](https://mrsl.roadmaptozero.com/MRSL2_0?guidance=1)

### Inferences
All points below are **[INFERENCE]** unless marked otherwise.

- **Rule 1 — amphiphilic anchor–buoy architecture.** The agent needs an anchor with real affinity for the hydrophobic indigo face and for the polyester surface, plus a hydrated buoy that prevents particle–particle and particle–fibre contact. Fully hydrophilic complexants (PVP, PVNO, plain polyacrylate, CMC) lack the anchor.
- **Rule 2 — anchor chemistry.** Prefer aromatic or planar groups that can π-stack on indigo and match polyester: oligo(ethylene/propylene terephthalate) or styryl/phenyl groups. H-bond acceptors (lactam, tertiary amide, ester C=O) are useful secondary sites. Long alkyl (C12–C18) or PVAc anchors are acceptable but less specific.
- **Rule 3 — buoy chemistry.** PEG or methyl-PEG chains from about 500 to 6,000 g/mol (about 11–136 EO) are the range spanned by the active systems found (mPEG500/750 in Boardman; PEG 1,000–10,000 in PET-POET; PEG 6000 in Sokalan HP 22). The hydrophilic fraction should be high enough for water solubility or dispersibility at 40–60 °C with a cloud point above bath temperature. As a working target (my estimate), 50–80 wt % EO, a Griffin-equivalent HLB of about 10–16.
- **Rule 4 — charge.** Non-ionic is the safe default: pH-independent across 4.5–7, tolerant of electrolyte, hardness and buffer, and kind to cellulase. If charge is added for electrosteric stabilisation, use sulfonate (sulfoisophthalate, sulfo end caps, sulfonated styrene), which is fully ionised at pH 4.5, in preference to carboxylate, which is only partly ionised there. Avoid cationic groups and amines that protonate at pH 4.5–5.5, because fibre surfaces are negative and commercial indigo carries anionic dispersant.
- **Rule 5 — molecular weight.** Target about 3,000–30,000 g/mol. Below about 1,000 the molecule behaves as a surfactant with reversible adsorption and weak steric layers. Above about 50,000–100,000 the polymer adds viscosity and can bridge particles to each other and to fibre at sub-saturation dosage [BACKGROUND – unverified for the bridging threshold], with no binding gain beyond about 20,000.
- **Rule 6 — address the protein route on cotton.** A polyester-type soil-release polymer protects polyester but has no cellulose affinity, and the cotton route is mediated by cellulase protein. Pair the polymer with an ethoxylated non-ionic surfactant, which reduces protein adsorption on cotton and indigo adsorption while raising cellulase activity. CMC is not a suitable cotton protector in the enzyme step because it is a cellulase substrate.
- **Rule 7 — dosage logic.** At an indigo load of the order of 0.1–1 g/L (my illustrative estimate), expect g/L-level agent dosing, not ppm. This is consistent with patent use levels of 0.1–1 wt % and with ethoxylate effects below 0.2 g/L, and it makes stoichiometric hosts such as β-cyclodextrin uneconomic.
- **Rule 8 — process robustness.** Ester linkages (terephthalate, PVAc) are adequately stable at pH 4.5–7 and 40–60 °C. Avoid polymers that are substrates for bath enzymes: CMC for cellulase, starch for residual amylase. Lipase-containing recipes could attack ester-based agents, which is worth checking. Prefer low-foam components (EO/PO-capped or branched ethoxylates) for drum machines.
- **Rule 9 — compliance.** Exclude APEO. For eco-label positioning prefer readily biodegradable surfactants (linear or lightly branched alcohol ethoxylates, APG) and polyester or polyether backbones that can hydrolyse. PVP, polycarboxylates and NSF condensates are generally regarded as poorly biodegradable [BACKGROUND – unverified].

**Candidate "ideal" structure (illustrative, by inference).** A methyl-PEG end-capped oligo(alkylene terephthalate), optionally with some 5-sulfoisophthalate units:

`CH3O–(CH2CH2O)n–[OC–C6H4–CO–O–CH2CH(R)–O]m–OC–C6H4–CO–(OCH2CH2)n–OCH3`

with R = H or CH3, n about 20–45 (PEG 1,000–2,000) and m about 3–10, giving roughly 2,500–6,500 g/mol by my arithmetic (higher with longer PEG or chain extension); for an anionic variant, replace 5–20 mol % of terephthalate with sodium 5-sulfoisophthalate. Formulate it with a branched or long-chain alcohol ethoxylate (iso-C13 with 8–10 EO, calculated HLB about 12.7–13.7, and/or C16–18 with about 25 EO, calculated HLB about 16.2). Optional additions are a minor amount of low-Mw sulfonated or carboxylated dispersant for pumice fines and hardness, and a lactam-bearing hydrophobically modified copolymer (PVP/VA type) as a secondary binder. The n and m values are my proposal within the patent ranges; they are not taken from a product data sheet.

**Alternative architecture (illustrative).** A comb copolymer with a styrene or benzyl methacrylate anchor block and mPEG (500–2,000) methacrylate side chains, with or without a minority of sulfonate monomer, in the same 5,000–30,000 g/mol range; or a PEG-g-PVAc of the Sokalan HP 22 type as a commercially available non-ionic baseline.

- Optimum Mw, EO fraction and anchor content for indigo specifically are not established by any data retrieved here.
- Requirements of ZDHC MRSL (v3.1 or later), bluesign, GOTS and OEKO-TEX for these polymer classes, including polymer biodegradability or residual-monomer criteria, were not verified from primary sources. Whether a newer ZDHC MRSL version than v3.1 is in force in November 2026 was not confirmed.
